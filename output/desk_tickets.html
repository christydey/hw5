Problem 10. Based on the final Problem 9 runs: Ticket 101 85124044c167, Ticket 102 5e8b62537b56, Ticket 103 883a2c0cd609. Checking went $3,400.00 → $2,560.00 → $160.00 → $160.00.

What surprised me

I was surprised by how much coordination the agents needed for tasks that first seemed simple. Paying an invoice was not just an Accounting task. In Ticket 101 the system had to understand the ticket, check inventory and why invoice #501 mattered, check the cash balance, pass the payment through the guardrails, and then wait for me to approve it. I liked seeing the agents specialize. But it also showed me that more agents do not automatically make a system more efficient. Sometimes the hand-offs added complexity that one capable agent with the same tools might have avoided.

My biggest takeaway is that the value of an agent system is not autonomy. It is coordination, specialization, and giving a human better information before a consequential decision.

1 · How well the agents performed

Ticket 101: Bulldog tee (Tauhid Zaman). Strong outcome, expensive path.

Ticket 101 was the best demonstration of the multi-agent system, because the problem really did cross several business functions. The customer wanted a size-S Bulldog tee that was out of stock, and the $840 invoice from Bulldog Print Co was preventing a reprint. Inventory, Accounting, Customer Service and the Boss each had a distinct job:

  • Inventory found 0 of the tee in size S (short 1). It found that Bulldog Print Co (5 lead days) would not ship while invoice #501 was open.
  • Accounting ran check_payment on invoice #501 twice. The second time it set reserved_amount=2400, so its proposal already accounted for the rent due in two days. It showed $2,560 left after the invoice, and $160 once rent was paid too.
  • Customer Service drafted an update to Tauhid that promised no arrival date.
  • The Boss recommended paying #501 only with my approval, and keeping the rent reserve.

What I liked most is that the agents did not simply spend the money. They investigated, worked out that paying #501 would unblock the order, and brought the $840 payment to me. That felt like the right line between AI decision support and human authority.

The path was expensive, though:

  • Six delegations. Inventory and Accounting each asked the other overlapping questions (Inventory → Accounting and Accounting → Inventory). Inventory ended up writing three reports.
  • Mostly repeated checks. The run made 24 model requests. Of its 16 MCP calls, 10 were repeats answered from the run's cache.
  • Two guardrail rejections. Inventory cited a tool name that doesn't exist (get_backorder_and_vendor_ship_status). Customer Service cited a teammate's report before it had asked that teammate.

The guardrails worked, but the agents still had to be corrected. I would grade 101 high on judgment and lower on efficiency.

Ticket 102: Rent due (Elm City Properties). Correct, but over-staffed.

The result was right:

  • Facilities used get_rent_due_and_cash_position to confirm the notice matched lease #1: $2,400, due 2026-09-02, two days after the shop date.
  • Accounting confirmed through check_payment that paying would leave $160.

This was also where the system felt most inefficient:

  • Duplicate question. Facilities had already sent the payment question to Accounting. The Boss then asked Accounting again and got the same answer.
  • Out-of-role attempt. Facilities tried to write the payment proposal itself, and the guardrail sent it back with "Only Accounting prepares payment proposals".

That took 14 model requests and 3 delegations to answer a question with a very short answer: is this rent legitimate, due, and affordable?

The human step mattered most here. After approving the $840 invoice, I had to decide whether to spend $2,400 of the $2,560 that was left. The AI correctly verified the payment was valid and technically affordable. But leaving the business with $160 is also a judgment about liquidity and priorities. I would not want an autonomous agent making that call without me.

Ticket 103: Bulk hoodie discount (Yale AI Club). Good research, inconsistent answer.

The research was solid:

  • Inventory's get_bulk_order_stock_and_pricing found 8 of the 20 size-M hoodies (short 12).
  • It used list_vendors to pick Bulldog Print Co as a restock source. It correctly said this was inferred from the vendor's specialty, since no table links products to vendors.
  • get_vendor_ship_status showed Bulldog could now ship, because I had paid invoice #501 for Ticket 101. One ticket's approval changed another ticket's facts, which I found interesting.

Every price discussed stayed above the $22 unit cost.

The weakness was consistency. Accounting told the Boss 15% off ($49.30 each). Later, answering Customer Service, it said 10% off ($52.20 each), and Customer Service's draft used 10%. The Boss noticed the conflict and told a human to settle the price before sending anything, which was honest but left the decision open. No restock was evaluated (check_payment was never called), and no cash moved.

The ticket is marked "resolved" because the team finished its run, not because the hoodie price was settled. I'd grade 103 as useful research with an unfinished decision.

3 · Would one agent with tools have been simpler?

For parts of these tasks, yes. A five-agent setup makes the most sense when a ticket genuinely crosses departments, as 101 did. For simpler tickets, the delegations and repeated checks create coordination overhead without adding much. The goal shouldn't be to involve as many agents as possible, but to use a specialist only when its expertise adds something.

  • Ticket 102 would have been simpler with one agent. The basic question was whether the $2,400 owed to Elm City Properties was legitimate, due and affordable. One agent with get_rent_due_and_cash_position and check_payment could have answered it and queued the approval. Instead it took 3 delegations, 14 model requests, about 38,600 input tokens, a guardrail rejection, and the same question asked of Accounting twice. inference I'm estimating, not measuring, that one agent would need only a handful of calls.
  • Ticket 103 shows a cost of specialization: inconsistency. Because Accounting was asked twice, by two different teammates, it produced two different discounts. That conflict exists only because the work was split up. A single agent would have produced one price.
  • Ticket 101 shows both sides. The specialists added real value. Inventory knew to tie invoice #501 to the reprint, and Accounting knew to hold back the $2,400 rent. But Inventory and Accounting asking each other overlapping questions, plus 10 repeated MCP calls, is overhead that one agent holding all the facts would avoid. The run used about 68,900 input tokens, largely because every hand-off re-sends prompts and context.

Specialization still bought things I value:

  • Separation of duties. Only Accounting can prepare a payment, and only Customer Service can write a customer draft. The guardrail stopped Facilities from writing a payment in 102.
  • Easier prompts. Each prompt only has to teach one set of rules.

Some of that could be achieved with one agent and per-tool permissions. If I redesigned it, I'd let the Boss handle simple, single-department tickets directly, and only bring in specialists for tickets like 101 that need several kinds of expertise.

4 · Three new problems the current team could solve

Each assumes the request arrives as a row in tickets. There's currently no route or tool for creating a ticket (see Q5).

A. Cash-flow prioritization between competing bills

The final balance of $160, after the $840 supplier invoice and $2,400 rent, shows why I'd want this. When several bills and purchases compete for limited cash, I want to see the consequences of different choices together, not each payment judged on its own: "If you pay rent and this supplier today, you will have $160 left."

  • Already supported. get_cash_and_obligations returns every open invoice and the next rent, plus cash_after_open_invoices_and_next_rent. check_payment takes reserved_amount, so Accounting can test payments in sequence. That's exactly what it did in Ticket 101 with reserved_amount=2400.
  • Already enforced. The payment guardrail re-checks proposals in priority order with cash reserved cumulatively.
  • Owners: Accounting leads; the Boss presents the options to me.
  • Limit: it only covers obligations already in the database. There's no forecasting, and no revenue is modelled.

B. A customer orders a Crest Mug, which is out of stock

CC-MUG-CREST (one size) has 0 in stock. Unlike the tee, its likely supplier isn't blocked.

  • Inventory uses get_backorder_and_vendor_block_status (stock 0, short 1) and list_vendors (Elm City Gifts, "mugs and small goods", 3 lead days). get_vendor_ship_status(2) confirms it has no open invoices.
  • Accounting runs check_payment(kind="purchase_order", sku="CC-MUG-CREST", qty=12, vendor_id=2). That costs 12 × $3.50 = $42, within the $160 in checking. It goes into my approval queue, and approving it records the payment through execute_approved_payment.
  • Customer Service drafts the reply.

C. Next month's rent notice, when the shop can't cover it

Lease #1 is now due 2026-10-02 for $2,400, and checking holds $160.

  • Facilities runs get_rent_due_and_cash_position, which would show checking going negative after rent.
  • Accounting's check_payment(lease_rent, 1) would come back refuse for insufficient cash. The approval would be stored as "blocked by safety check", with no Approve button.
  • The Boss would escalate to me with the shortfall.

The team can't create money, but it would handle the ticket correctly. It would warn me early and could never overdraw the account.

The common thread is that I'd keep the same division of labor I liked in these runs. Agents research, check, recommend and prepare. A human keeps control of anything consequential: money, prices, and messages that leave the shop.
